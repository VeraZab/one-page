I've been experimenting with subagents lately, and somewhere in the middle of setting one up it hit me: I wasn't sure what actually separates a subagent from a skill. Claude Code hands you both, they're both just markdown files of instructions, and at a glance they look like the same tool wearing two hats.

Here's how I've come to think about them.

The thing that separates them isn't capability. It's whether you need to steer the work as it happens, or judge it at checkpoints. A skill runs inside the conversation you're already in — you watch each step land and can nudge it mid-stream. A subagent goes off on its own and comes back with a result you review.

I felt the difference the hard way. I'd sent a slow page off to a specialist subagent for a refactor, and the work came back as clean commits I could just skim and approve. Perfect. Then I wanted one row to look *just a little* different — and suddenly the same distance that made the refactor clean was the problem. The subagent was off on its own, every question routing back through the main agent and back out again. For the refactor, that distance was exactly what I wanted. For nudging one row's contrast, it meant waiting on a round-trip every single time I wanted to see what changed.

## The real difference is judgment

There's a subtler thing underneath the steering question. A skill runs on *your* context — it has your decisions and your assumptions, which means it also quietly inherits your blind spots. A subagent never saw any of that. It gets a brief and figures out how to get there — loading whatever skills it needs, deciding what to tackle first, pushing back when something seems off. That independence is the whole point: it can reach a conclusion you wouldn't have, because nothing you assumed is nudging it.

So don't open with "which one's better?" Open with:

> Do I need to steer this while it runs, or judge it at checkpoints?

## When you need to steer: reach for a skill

- **The work is visual or iterative.** Rendering tweaks, layout, wording — these are tight little feedback loops, and every trip out to a subagent adds a relay you have to wait on.
- **There's a human step in the middle.** A login flow that ends with "ask me to confirm the code" needs an actual someone to ask. Any question it has routes back through the main agent first, and that's a long way around for a yes or no.
- **Each step leans on something you just decided here.** A "write the PR" skill that builds the summary from what this conversation actually knows — what changed, why, which boxes are genuinely ticked — only works with all of that still in the room.
- **It's a short recipe, not a job.** If the whole thing is "run these steps in order," that's a skill. If it needs to figure out how to approach the problem first, that's a subagent.

## When you only want the result: send a subagent

- **It's big and file-heavy, and you want the outcome, not the noise.** A dozen files and ten test runs would bury the higher-level conversation you're trying to have.
- **The domain has rules worth enforcing.** A specialist that owns how a thing is built will push back when a request cuts against the grain — which is exactly what you want from someone who knows the area better than you do in the moment.
- **You want a genuine second opinion.** A read-only reviewer that never saw your reasoning can't share your blind spots — that's the feature.
- **It can run in parallel.** One half of a feature can get built in its own session from a tight little contract while you build the other half.

And one rule that beats all of these: if a ticket or a teammate names a specialist, start with the specialist.

## The pattern I landed on: same rules, delivered both ways

Here's the bit that made it all click for me: the knowledge doesn't have to pick a side.

I keep the actual rules — which component to use, where a given change belongs, what never to do — in one standalone skill. The specialist subagent loads that exact same skill before it starts, then adds what only an agent can carry: a working method, its own tool limits, a review mode. One source of truth, two ways to deliver it.

So a task now tends to go:

1. **Big reshapes go to the subagent.** New data source, new layout, a page loading way too much. I review the commits, not the process.
2. **Tweaks I want to watch happen inline, with the skill loaded.** Same guardrails, except I keep steering.
3. **The subagent does a read-only review at the end.** Fresh eyes catch whatever the quick inline tweaks quietly broke.

## Decide before you start reading

One timing rule matters more than the rest: decide whether to delegate *before* you start reading the code. Once you've done the research inline, the context you've built up will always argue for staying inline — you've already got the files open, the path traced, the shape in your head. That's how an hour disappears into work a specialist was set up to own.

## What each one costs

Neither is free, and it helps to see the bill up front.

A **skill** spends your context window — every rule it drops in is tokens the main conversation now carries — and it inherits your blind spots, because it runs on the same assumptions you walked in with.

A **subagent** costs differently:

- **Briefing is real work.** The specialist can't open your ticket tracker or read your mind. You paste in the gap list, the paths, the constraints — and a good brief takes a few real minutes.
- **You become the switchboard.** Every question it has comes back through you, and your answer goes back out the same way. For a long back-and-forth, that gets old fast.
- **You see the results, not the reasoning.** Commits, a hot reload, a final diff — sure. The thinking that got there — no.
- **A vague brief gets a confident answer to the wrong question.** With nothing to anchor it, a subagent will happily, thoroughly solve the thing *next to* what you meant.

## A rule of thumb

When you're not sure, sort it by that one question:

- **Steering, a human step, or decisions from this conversation → skill.**
- **Big, specialized, needs fresh eyes, or can run in parallel → subagent.**
- **Best of both: write the knowledge once as a skill, have the subagent load it, and use whichever shape fits the moment.**

The capability is the same either way. What you're really choosing is how close you want to stay to the work.
